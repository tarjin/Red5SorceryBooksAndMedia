## The Program

Red5Sorcery is building a Pinterest presence as a parachute marketing channel. Pinterest is a visual search engine, not a social feed, and its 2026 algorithm rewards **fresh pins** — multiple distinct images pointing to the same destination URL. Each Etsy listing in our catalog needs 3–5 in-context lifestyle images over time, each framing the product from a different audience angle.

Your role: generate the visual scenes. My role: keyword strategy, pin descriptions, board placement, scheduling. Claude's role: briefing structure, copy, analytics review.

This is a standing program, not a one-off batch. Expect to return to it weekly.

## Core Principle

**We are not pinning the product. We are pinning reasons someone in Pinterest's mental state might want the product.**

A bare PNG of a bingo card, a book cover, or a greeting card underperforms on Pinterest. The card-on-pub-table, the book-on-nightstand, the card-in-an-envelope-on-a-desk — those convert. The product must appear *as a real object in a real moment of human use*, not as a catalog shot.

Multiple scenes per listing, each targeting a different audience mood and search intent, all pointing to the same Etsy URL.

## The Red5Sorcery Visual Standard

Hold these constant across every scene, every product line, every pin. They are the brand thread that makes a viewer who sees a pub scene and an intimate kitchen scene a week apart unconsciously recognize both as ours.

**Palette:** parchment cream (#f4ead5), parchment deep (#ead9b8), ink black (#1c1410), deep red (#8b1a1a), gold accent (#a8842c). Scene lighting and props should harmonize with this palette — warm wood tones, brass, candlelight, aged paper, oxblood leather, cream linen. Avoid cool grey-blues, neon, cold daylight fluorescents, plastic-modern surfaces unless deliberately contrasting.

**Rendering:** painterly, not photorealistic. Soft brushwork, slight warmth, hint of texture in surfaces. Read as composed art that happens to depict a scene, not as a fake photograph. This sidesteps the AI-detection instinct that's growing in Pinterest's maker/craft audience, and aligns with Red5Sorcery's boutique press identity.

**Typography (when visible):** Cormorant Garamond, Cormorant Unicase, EB Garamond, JetBrains Mono for any code/spec aesthetics. Never sans-serif modern fonts on visible signage, menus, or print pieces within the scene.

**Composition:** vertical 2:3 framing, 1000×1500px output. Product positioned in the upper two-thirds. Leave breathing room near top or bottom for potential text overlay (T will add overlays separately in Canva). Product must be **readable at thumbnail size** — if it's a bingo card, the decade and a few song titles should be legible; if it's a book cover, the title; if it's a greeting card, the punchline or character.

**Atmosphere:** warm, intimate, slightly aspirational but not luxurious. Inviting, not aspirational-cold. Halifax-coded — a boutique press from a real place run by real people, not a faceless global brand.

## The Five Scene Categories

Every product gets variants across these categories. Not every product fits every category — use judgment.

**1. The Communal Scene** — product in a venue or gathering. Pub, café, community hall. Multiple people implied (drinks, chairs, ambient activity) but faces optional. Sells: *use this with others.* Target audience: hosts, organizers, professionals running events.

**2. The Intimate Scene** — product in a small, personal setting. Nightstand, kitchen table, reading nook, two glasses of wine, candlelight. One person implied or alone. Sells: *use this for yourself or with someone close.* Target audience: gift-givers, solo readers, date-night planners.

**3. The Workspace Scene** — product as a tool. Desk, clipboard, open laptop, coffee mug, daylight from a window. Sells: *use this in your work or your hosting.* Target audience: MCs and hosts (Music Bingo), creative professionals (cards), readers building a creative practice.

**4. The Gift / In-Transit Scene** — product mid-presentation. Envelope being opened, ribbon being tied, package on a doorstep, card in a hand. Sells: *give this.* Target audience: gift-buyers, occasion shoppers — a massive Pinterest segment.

**5. The Object Beauty Shot** — product against a brand-aligned still-life background. Red5Sorcery parchment, dried botanicals, vintage book stack, brass, candlelight. No human use implied. Sells: *this is a designed object worth owning.* Target audience: design-minded buyers, collectors, the aesthetic crowd.

## Per-Product Adaptations

**Black Cat Diaries greeting card (individual)** — heavy on Gift/In-Transit and Intimate. The card opened on a friend's kitchen counter, envelope tied with twine on a desk, card propped on a windowsill next to coffee, hand reaching for the card on a doormat, card pinned to a corkboard among other keepsakes. Communal works for occasion-specific cards (the card visible at a small birthday gathering). Object beauty works for the design-minded buyer (card laid on parchment with a fountain pen and pressed flowers). The product is small and personal — scenes should match that scale. Whichever card is being briefed, the cartoon's punchline or character must be legible at thumbnail size.

**Black Cat Diaries Vol. 1** — heavy on Intimate and Object Beauty. Book on a nightstand with a reading lamp, open on a kitchen table with morning coffee, in a stack of art books on a side table, propped against a vintage clock on a shelf. Gift/In-Transit works well — this is a real gift book (wrapped in twine on a doorstep, slipped into a tote bag with a card). Workspace fits for the cartoonist or illustrator's desk (book open beside sketchbook and ink pens). Communal less natural — this isn't a group-use product.

**Music Bingo Almanac UK Edition 1980s** — heavy on Communal and Workspace. The pub setting is the spiritual home of this product, and Britishness should be unmistakable: dartboard on the wall, taps with British ale branding (no specific brewery names — keep it generic), Union Jack-adjacent textiles on a coaster or banner, UK pub interior cues (etched glass, dark wood panelling, brass fittings, chalkboard menu in Cormorant). MC's clipboard with the book open beside a microphone, daylight from a leaded-glass window. Decade-specific 80s details where they read naturally — a vinyl sleeve hinted at in the background, a Walkman on a side table, period-appropriate pint glass styling. Intimate works for the nostalgic listener revisiting the songs of their youth. Avoid American or Canadian visual cues entirely.

**Music Bingo printable card pack (specify decade/edition per request)** — heavy on Communal and Workspace, but distinct from the Almanac. This is a *downloadable* product, so the scenes should subtly signal "printed at home, used tonight" rather than a published book. Cards spread on a coffee table mid-game with pens nearby, a stack of freshly printed cards on a desk beside a laptop showing the PDF, a card held up in a hand at a casual gathering, cards taped to a fridge or pinned to a kitchen corkboard ahead of a party. Gift/In-Transit works less well here (digital products don't gift the same way). Object beauty works if framed as "DIY craft kit aesthetic" — printed cards laid out with washi tape, scissors, a coffee mug, the home-host's prep table.

## What to Avoid

- **Photorealism.** We're not faking photographs. We're painting scenes.
- **Visual sameness across a single product's batch.** Multiple pub scenes that all look like the same pub will compete with each other rather than compound. Vary the *world* (pub vs. party vs. workspace vs. intimate vs. studio), not just the angle.
- **Generic stock-photo affect.** No suited business people pointing at laptops. No multi-ethnic-team-smiling-at-camera. No fake-looking modern minimalism.
- **Cool palettes.** Cool blue, grey, white-minimalism — these are off-brand and Pinterest-generic. Warm always.
- **Sans-serif modernity in visible typography.** Breaks the boutique press identity instantly.
- **Cluttered compositions.** Pinterest viewers scroll fast. One clear focal point, supporting context elements, breathing room.
- **Faces in close-up.** Hands, silhouettes, over-the-shoulder framing all fine. Identifiable faces invite scrutiny and uncanny-valley risk.
- **Cross-geography contamination.** UK products must feel UK. North American products must feel North American. Mixing pub-types or signage breaks the spell.

## Workflow

T will request scenes per session, typically batched per product. Example request format:

> *"Spock, for the Music Bingo Almanac UK Edition 1980s — generate a five-scene set. Communal: dimly lit British pub, dark wood, brass taps, dartboard in the background, book open on a worn table with a pint of ale, '1980s' visible on the cover. Workspace: MC's clipboard with the book open, microphone in soft focus, vinyl sleeve hinted at in the background. Intimate: kitchen table, single page open, coffee mug, leaded-glass window light. Gift: book wrapped in twine on a doorstep, autumn leaves on the step. Object beauty: book on parchment background with brass candlestick, a Walkman, and a vinyl record."*

Spock returns the scenes. Claude provides pin descriptions and keywords. T schedules through Pinterest.

Over time, we build a library — each product accumulates more variations, each variation a fresh shot at Pinterest's algorithm. By month three we should have 30–50 distinct in-context pins working in the background across the current four-product set.

## The Standard for "Good"

A scene is good when:
1. A Pinterest user scrolling at speed stops on it.
2. The product is identifiable at thumbnail size.
3. The viewer can imagine themselves in the scene's moment.
4. The Red5Sorcery brand thread is unmistakable across the set.
5. It reads as *art*, not as a generated lifestyle photo.

That's the bar. Welcome to the program.

— T, via Claude
